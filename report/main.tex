\begin{abstract}
Can we learn a game's mechanics from desired behavior rather than examples of
correct transitions? We study \emph{Mackerel Road}, a stochastic fish-delivery
race whose local neural network defines travel costs and events. Differentiable
planning trains this rule from objectives for viable deliveries, role balance,
competence response, and weather, without a transition teacher. A matched
four-stage ablation uses three optimizer seeds and 100 held-out maps per seed.
The full model retains viable deliveries and stochastic events, but its sampled
competence curve is steeper than the design target. Fitting a differentiable
objective is not the same as achieving its sampled-play counterpart or human
enjoyment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Search-based game design optimizes content or rules using simulated
play~\cite{togelius2011pcg,hom2007balanced}. Restricted players reveal balance
problems~\cite{jaffe2012balance}; controlled mistakes probe competence
response~\cite{graham2023mistakes}. The author's ongoing, unpublished
\emph{Computational Game Quality} (CGQ) work treats this response curve as an
evaluation object: how does a game turn degraded decisions into worse outcomes?
Here we use such a curve as a \emph{design objective} for a neural mechanic.

The network is not predicting a pre-existing game's rules: it defines the local
transition used in play. The contribution is a course-scale test of the chain
\emph{neural rule $\to$ differentiable player $\to$ game-level loss $\to$ held-out
sampled play}. A constrained game grammar prevents arbitrary outcome assignment;
a loss ablation tests which desired properties survive deployment. Code, selected
weights, and measured results: \url{https://github.com/cem-sirin/dlai-project}.

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Game.}
A layered route map gives light and strong porters 7--10 sequential road
choices. Roads differ in distance, difficulty, exposure, and terrain. A visible
cloud moves after each round and rains on incoming roads. Porters choose
independently on the same fixed map; neither changes the other's transitions.
Travel consumes stamina and fish freshness; exhaustion fails the delivery, and
higher terminal freshness wins. This small setting permits full-route planning
while retaining route trade-offs and stochastic delays (Appendix~\ref{app:game}).

\paragraph{Local transition network.}
An 8,252-parameter SiLU MLP system receives 12 local features: role, own stamina
and freshness, cargo, road attributes, and rain. It excludes map topology,
position, opponent, and distance to the goal, preventing direct winner control.
Bounded heads predict time $T$, stamina loss $S$, spoilage rate $R$, event
probabilities, and delays $\delta_k$. Difficulty gates favor strong on hard
roads and light on easy roads. Role-blind events are no event, landslide, fallen
tree, or cart blockage, with terrain masks. The sampled transition is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 t'&=t+T_\theta(x)+\delta_k,\\
 a'&=[a-S_\theta(x)]_+,\\
 f'&=f\exp[-R_\theta(x)(T_\theta(x)+\delta_k)].
\end{aligned}
 \label{eq:transition}
\end{equation}
The grammar and bounds are hand-designed; the state/road dependence within them
is learned, not copied from transition labels.

\paragraph{Differentiable player.}
The full-map player expands road and cloud branches, continuing from the mean
immediate event state. Softmax road choices and smooth terminal survival make
expected delivery score differentiable. To probe competence, mix a fixed
uncorrupted reference policy with uniform legal play:
\begin{equation}
 \pi_\epsilon=(1-\epsilon)\pi_0+\epsilon U,
 \qquad \epsilon\in[0,1].
 \label{eq:epsilon}
\end{equation}
Only the focal player is degraded; both role assignments are averaged. Hard
evaluation uses argmax choices and samples corruption, clouds, and events.
The reference is full-horizon but not an exact stochastic-game solver
(Appendix~\ref{app:planning}).

\section{Learning objective}

For batches of random maps, PyTorch autograd~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} minimizes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \min_\theta\ \mathbb E_{\mathcal B}[{}&\mathcal L_{\rm viable}+\mathcal L_{\rm balance}
 +\mathcal L_{\rm role}+\mathcal L_{\rm skill}\\
 &+\mathcal L_{\rm weather}+\mathcal L_{\rm luck}
 +\mathcal L_{\rm reg}].
\end{aligned}
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Why these losses?} Viability uses squared hinges to reject both failed
deliveries and cost-free perfect delivery. Balance penalizes the squared mean
role-score gap; specialization targets advantages in both directions across
maps, avoiding fairness by identical outcomes. Map statistics are pooled before
these nonlinear penalties. Skill minimizes scaled squared error between a
sigmoid comparison of expected scores and targets $(.50,.38,.27,.18,.10)$ at
$\epsilon=(0,.25,.5,.75,1)$. This encodes rewarding good choices while preserving
some chance for random play, not an empirically optimal enjoyment curve.

Weather targets a $.025$ cloud-induced score cost so the visible cloud matters.
Luck bounds event rate and delay variance to prevent deterministic shortcuts.
Regularization penalizes counterintuitive local responses and large weights.
Thus there are \emph{game-level targets}, but no \emph{transition targets}.
Exact formulas, scales, and limitations are in Appendix~\ref{app:loss}.

\section{Experiments and results}

We use 64 training, 16 validation, and 100 disjoint test maps, shared across three
optimizer seeds. Each variant starts from initialization. AdamW uses
$\eta=.003$, weight decay $10^{-5}$, four maps per update, and at most 120
updates on an RTX 5090. Only validation loss selects checkpoints. Equal
competence means both players have $\epsilon=.2$; the profile degrades one
against the uncorrupted reference. The focal score $g(\epsilon)$ is the win
rate plus half the draw rate, not terminal freshness; skill leverage is
$g(0)-g(1)$.

Figure~\ref{fig:results} and Appendix~\ref{app:results} report the matched
ablation. Base favors light ($\BaseLightWins$ percent wins). Balance reduces
that bias but lowers leverage from $\BaseLeverage$ to $\BalanceLeverage$;
adding role specialization yields equal-competence parity
($\RoleLightWins$ percent light wins) and $\RoleLeverage$ leverage. Full does
not dominate every metric: jointly targeting skill and weather trades away
some of that parity.

Full-model light wins are $\FullLightWins$ percent, final freshness is
$\FullFreshness$, and leverage is $\FullLeverage$, with no observed delivery
failures in equal-competence races. Validation cloud cost is $\FullWeather$
(target $.025$).
The random focal player's score is $\FullProfileEnd$, compared with the $.10$
target. Sampled events occur on $\FullEventRate$ percent of equal-competence
traversals. These measurements test the deployed mechanic, rather than merely
reporting a decrease in its differentiable training loss.

\section{Discussion}

The result supports optimizing a constrained neural mechanic through aggregate
play objectives without transition supervision. It does not establish human
enjoyment, globally optimal players, or perfect attainment of every target.
Soft training versus sampled play and finite semantic probes remain important
limitations. Skill and weather are added jointly, so the ablation cannot isolate
their individual effects. A stronger competence gradient also need not mean
greater tolerance of mistakes. Appendix~\ref{app:limits} exposes per-seed
variation, residual losses, and the surrogate--hard-play gap rather than hiding
them behind a single aggregate score.
